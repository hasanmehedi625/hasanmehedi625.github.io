Research

Predicting wildfire-induced power outages with nighttime lights data

MS thesis, Washington State University · Advisor: Dr. Ji Yun Lee

Nighttime light radiance drops when the grid goes down, but the signal is noisy, viewing-angle dependent, and only loosely tied to how many customers actually lost service. I am building a satellite-based framework that learns a pixel-level deviation score from VIIRS nighttime light imagery and pools those pixels, weighted by customer count, into county-level estimates of the fraction of customers out — supervised against EAGLE-I outage records.

  • Hazard-agnostic detector first trained across hurricane events, then transferred to wildfire settings where outage cause is genuinely ambiguous.
  • Processing pipelines built for 15 hurricane events across FL, LA, TX, NC, VA, GA and Puerto Rico, including Irma, Michael, Ida, Harvey, Florence, Beryl and Helene.
  • Per-pixel angular correction of day–night band radiance using sensor zenith angle, nadir-normalized with a quadratic model.
  • Leave-one-event-out validation with multi-seed ensembles and regime-stratified error reporting, so performance is measured on storms the model has never seen.

Spatial structure of outages and the power grid network

In progress

Outage pixels should cluster along the physical power network rather than scatter at random. I test this by combining substation, transmission line and road geometry with density-based clustering of continuous outage probability, then learning a correction term from each pixel's signed distance to its cluster boundary.

Distinguishing wildfire-driven outages from PSPS and cascading failure

In progress

Not every outage in a fire-affected region is caused by fire damage. Some are preemptive public safety power shutoffs; others propagate into regions the hazard never reached, through grid interdependency. I am using a vision-language model together with utility outage-cause records to separate these mechanisms.
